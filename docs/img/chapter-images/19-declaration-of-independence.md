A wide-format document portrait illustration (16:9 aspect ratio) depicting the Declaration
of Independence as a physical historical artifact — the actual parchment document held by
the National Archives in Washington, D.C. — styled as a US history textbook illustration
that makes the document feel tangible and real while illuminating its content, context,
and the gap between its ideals and the lived reality of 1776.

COMPOSITION — DOCUMENT AS PRIMARY SUBJECT:
The Declaration parchment fills approximately three-quarters of the frame, displayed at a
slight angle on a dark archival surface, brilliantly but carefully lit to reveal every
detail of its current physical state. The remaining quarter provides contextual framing.

THE DOCUMENT ITSELF — PHYSICAL REALITY:
- The actual Declaration of Independence (National Archives copy, signed August 2, 1776)
  is rendered in its TRUE current condition — which is significantly more faded than most
  textbook images show: the parchment is large (24.5 by 29.75 inches), made from vellum
  (calfskin), and the ink — iron gall on parchment — has severely faded over 250 years,
  particularly due to 19th-century exposure to light (it was displayed facing a window for
  decades in the Patent Office and State Department)
- The fading is itself historically significant: most of the text is barely legible to the
  naked eye; the most visible portions are the large signatures in the lower half of the
  document (especially John Hancock's famously bold signature)
- The parchment: warm cream-ivory with age-brown tinting at the edges; slight wrinkling
  and creasing from its history; a faint foxing (age spots) in places; the surface texture
  of real vellum — slightly translucent when lit from behind

THE TEXT (as visible):
- The opening engrossed header: "IN CONGRESS, JULY 4, 1776." in large decorative lettering
  — still relatively legible at the top
- "The unanimous Declaration of the thirteen united States of America" — in ornate
  copperplate script, visible but faded to warm brown
- The main text body: dense columns of 18th-century copperplate script; the first lines
  of the preamble are rendered legible enough to read the famous phrases:
  "We hold these truths to be self-evident, that all men are created equal..."
- The signature columns in the lower half: 56 signatures in varying sizes and clarity;
  John Hancock's signature (largest, most central, in the president's position) remains
  the most visible; Charles Thomson (Secretary) counter-signed
- The signatures are grouped by colony/state delegation, south to north — Georgia
  at the lower left, New Hampshire at upper right of the signature block

LIGHTING:
- The document is illuminated by museum-quality conservation lighting: cool, even,
  UV-filtered — the kind used in the National Archives Rotunda
- A slight directional quality from the upper-right catches the texture of the vellum
  and the slight relief of the handwritten ink strokes
- The parchment glows with its own warm ivory light against a dark surrounding field
