EXPLANATORY TEXT (white serif, museum-exhibit style):
"Benjamin Franklin, Pennsylvania Gazette, May 9, 1754

Context: Published on the eve of the French and Indian War (1754–1763), this woodcut
accompanied Franklin's call for colonial unity at the Albany Congress. The cartoon
argued that the colonies, like the segments of the snake, would perish separately but
survive together.

The Superstition It Invokes: A popular folk belief held that a snake cut in pieces
would reassemble and come back to life if the pieces were reunited before sunset —
making Franklin's image simultaneously a threat ('die') and a promise ('join and live').

Afterlife: The cartoon was revived during the American Revolution (1765–1783) as a
symbol of resistance to British rule, appearing on flags, newspaper mastheads, and
broadsides across the colonies — often with 'UNITE OR DIE' as the motto."
