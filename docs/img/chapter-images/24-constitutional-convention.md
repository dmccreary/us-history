Philadelphia, during the summer of 1787 — styled as a historically grounded US history
textbook illustration that conveys the genuine drama, difficulty, and human complexity of
the four-month closed-door debate that created the United States Constitution.

COMPOSITION — THE ASSEMBLY ROOM IN DEBATE:
The scene depicts a moment of active, contentious debate — NOT the final formal signing
scene, which is well-documented elsewhere, but rather the working legislative session
at the height of the great compromise negotiations of June–July 1787. The viewpoint is
from the gallery level (there was no public gallery — this is a slightly elevated,
observer-position angle that gives the viewer a clear view of the full room and its dynamics).

THE ASSEMBLY ROOM — PERIOD SETTING:
- The same Assembly Room where the Declaration was signed, now reorganized for a
  legislative working session: chairs and small delegate tables arranged in a semicircle
  or horse-shoe pattern facing the presiding chair; the room is close and warm in
  the Philadelphia July heat; the windows are CLOSED — the delegates voted to keep the
  windows shut to prevent eavesdropping and to maintain secrecy (the debates were
  kept secret until Madison's notes were published in 1840)
- This creates a specific physical atmosphere: the room is stuffy, the August heat
  is palpable in the delegates' loosened cravats and slightly damp clothing; candles
  provide supplemental light even in daytime because the closed shutters reduce sunlight
- The presiding chair (the "rising sun" chair) at the front: George Washington seated
  in it — a crucial compositional anchor

GEORGE WASHINGTON — THE PRESIDING OFFICER (FRONT CENTER):
- Washington occupies the ornate carved presiding chair, slightly elevated on a low
  platform, at the far end of the room from the viewer's perspective
- Age 55 in 1787; still powerfully built; in his civilian dark blue coat; his expression
  shows the strain and authority of presiding over four months of contentious debate
  without expressing his own opinions (convention rules required the presiding officer
  to remain neutral)
- His mere presence gave the proceedings legitimacy; delegates who might have walked
  out stayed because they did not want to embarrass Washington
- His hands rest on the arms of the chair; he is watching the current debate with
  contained intensity

KEY FIGURES IN ACTIVE DEBATE:
- James Madison (standing to speak, left-center): small, slight, age 36 — Madison was
  5'4" and often underestimated physically; he compensates with intellectual precision;
  he holds notes (he sat in front to take his famous detailed notes, which are the
  primary record of the debates); his expression is earnest and focused as he argues
  his position on representation
- Benjamin Franklin (seated, center-left): age 81 — the oldest delegate; he is visibly
  aged and occasionally had to be carried to sessions in a sedan chair; he sits quietly,
  perhaps dozing or thinking, but his presence carries enormous weight; his occasional

- Alexander Hamilton (standing to speak near the right side): young, age 32, energetic;

- Gouverneur Morris (standing, right): tall, aristocratic bearing; he had a wooden

  Convention and would write the final preamble ("We the People...")
- Several Southern delegates visible on the right side of the room: Charles Cotesworth

  well-dressed — he introduced the Fugitive Slave Clause); their expressions show
  the hard-bargaining posture of men who had their own conditions for signing

OPTIONAL CAPTION (lower right, dark panel, white serif text):
"Philadelphia, summer 1787. 55 delegates from 12 states (Rhode Island refused to attend)
