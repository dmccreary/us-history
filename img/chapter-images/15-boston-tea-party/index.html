    A dramatic, wide-format historical illustration (16:9 aspect ratio) depicting the Boston Tea Party on the night of December 16, 1773 — styled as a historically grounded US history textbook illustration that shows the event's real character: not a spontaneous mob action but an organized political protest carried out by members of the Sons of Liberty, many of them prominent Bostonians in thin disguise.

    COMPOSITION — GRIFFIN'S WHARF, BOSTON HARBOR, NIGHT: The scene takes place at Griffin's Wharf (now buried under landfill) in Boston Harbor. Three ships are moored side by side at the wharf: the Dartmouth, the Eleanor, and the Beaver — three merchant vessels of the East India Company carrying 342 chests of British East India Company tea. The viewpoint is slightly elevated, looking down the length of the wharf toward the harbor mouth, so the full scope of the operation is visible.

    THE THREE SHIPS (CENTER AND BACKGROUND): - Three typical late 18th-century brig-rigged merchant vessels (approximately 110–130 tons) moored tightly at the wharf; their rigging and spars are visible against the night sky - Deck lights (lanterns) illuminate the working areas of each deck - On each ship: groups of men (approximately 50 total, spread across all three vessels) systematically working — using block-and-tackle to hoist chests from below decks, then swinging them to men standing at the rail, who use hatchets to break them open and dump the tea into the harbor water below

    THE FOREGROUND — GRIFFIN'S WHARF: - The wooden wharf planking in the foreground: slick with harbor spray and December cold - A crowd of Boston civilians watching from the wharf — approximately 2,000 Bostonians watched the event; they are shown as a crowd of men, women, and a few children wrapped in winter coats, watching in tense, mostly silent observation (notably, the crowd was orderly — there was no riot, no looting, no other property damage, almost no noise) - The crowd is NOT celebrating wildly — several figures have serious, watchful expressions; some look anxious; this was a momentous, potentially treasonous act and everyone knew it - A few British soldiers visible at the edge of the crowd — watching but NOT intervening (Governor Hutchinson had ordered them not to, fearing a larger riot) - Several lanterns on the wharf illuminate the crowd with warm amber light against the cold December darkness

    THE PARTICIPANTS: - The men aboard the ships are dressed in hastily improvised "Mohawk warrior" disguises: blankets or coarse cloth wrapped around their bodies; soot and burnt cork darkening their faces; some wear feathers stuck into their hats — the disguises were thin and most Bostonians recognized the participants (including the young Paul Revere) - The disguises served a political rather than practical purpose: they allowed participants to claim later they "didn't know" who was responsible, while also symbolically claiming American (not British) identity - The participants work systematically and calmly — no drinking, no fighting, no looting of any other cargo; when one participant tried to pocket a small amount of tea, the others stopped him - Several participants are visible using short-handled axes (hatchets) to break open the wooden chests before dumping them - One participant carries a broom — historically, a participant swept the deck clean of any spilled tea after the operation, an act of deliberate political symbolism (the protest was against the tea tax system, not against property destruction for its own sake)

    THE HARBOR AND TEA: - Boston Harbor at night: dark cold water reflecting the ship lanterns in shimmering orange-gold ripples - Visible in the water below the ship rails: broken wooden chest fragments floating; tea spreading across the water surface in spreading dark stains; the cumulative 342 chests (90,000 pounds of tea, worth £18,000 — roughly $1.7 million today) are slowly releasing into the tidal water - The quantity of tea is visually significant — the water surface near the ships is literally covered in floating tea leaves and broken chest fragments

    ATMOSPHERE: - Cold December night: breath visible as vapor in the lantern light; participants' clothing appropriate to New England winter; frost on the wharf planking - Partial moon visible through clouds, providing faint natural light over the harbor - The scene is strangely quiet and orderly for such a radical act — convey this through the posture and expressions of both participants and observers

    HISTORICAL ACCURACY NOTES: - The "war whoop" and theatrical staging of later myth is absent; this was businesslike - No alcohol visible — the Sons of Liberty kept the event strictly controlled - The "Mohawk" disguise was not culturally specific: it was generic "Indian" costume, a political statement about American (not British) identity, not an imitation of any specific indigenous people - Many participants were young tradespeople (apprentices, craftsmen); some were prominent merchants — showing that colonial resistance cut across economic classes

    OPTIONAL CAPTION (lower right, dark panel, white serif text): "December 16, 1773 — Boston Harbor. Members of the Sons of Liberty, thinly disguised, destroy 342 chests of East India Company tea worth £18,000. The act was deliberate, organized, and almost entirely nonviolent — no person was harmed, no other property touched. Parliament responded with the Coercive Acts (1774), which united the colonies against Britain as nothing before had done."

    TEXTBOOK ILLUSTRATION STYLE: Painterly nocturnal realism combining the drama of Romanticism with historical accuracy — in the tradition of American history illustration by Howard Pyle and the Brandywine school. Rich palette of deep harbor-blue, warm amber lantern-light, and the cold blue-black of a December New England night.
