    A dramatic, wide-format historical illustration (16:9 aspect ratio) depicting the siege and surrender at Yorktown, Virginia, October 19, 1781 — the decisive final campaign of the American Revolutionary War — styled as a historically grounded US history textbook illustration that conveys the multinational character of the American victory and the complex human realities of surrender.

    COMPOSITION — THE SURRENDER CEREMONY ON THE SURRENDER FIELD: The scene depicts the formal British surrender ceremony at the surrender field on the Yorktown- Hampton Road, October 19, 1781, at approximately 2 PM. The viewpoint is a wide establishing shot showing both the approaching British and German troops and the two lines of victorious allied forces.

    THE SURRENDER FIELD — WIDE VIEW: A broad Virginia field in mid-October: the grass is dry and golden; the leaves on the oaks and hickories at the field's edge are turning amber and red; a clear, bright autumn afternoon.

    Two long parallel lines of allied troops stretch the length of the frame: - LEFT LINE: French troops of Rochambeau's army — approximately 8,000 French soldiers in immaculate white uniforms (the Régiment Bourbonnais in white with black facings; the Régiment Deux-Ponts in white with yellow facings); their arms are presented with parade precision; their regimental colors flying in the afternoon breeze; the French officers mounted on well-appointed horses in French cavalry style - RIGHT LINE: Continental Army troops — approximately 5,500 American soldiers; their appearance is more varied: many Continental regiments now have standardized blue coats with state-specific facings (Virginia in blue with white; Maryland in blue with red; New England regiments in varied states); but some troops still show the patchwork appearance of an army that has always been under-supplied; the contrast with the French is deliberately visible

    GEORGE WASHINGTON AND ROCHAMBEAU (CENTER BACKGROUND): - Washington and the Comte de Rochambeau are mounted side by side at the center end of the two lines — equals in the victory, visually positioned as co-commanders - Washington: tall on his gray horse, in dark blue Continental general's coat with buff facings, buff breeches; composed, dignified; this is the vindication of everything since Boston in 1775 - Rochambeau: in French general's white coat with gold epaulettes; similarly composed; a slight mutual acknowledgment between the two men

    THE BRITISH SURRENDER PROCESSION (CENTER): - The British and German (Hessian) troops march between the two allied lines toward the surrender point, approximately 8,000 men surrendering - The troops are marching with their muskets shouldered — traditionally, surrendering armies would "ground arms" (stack and surrender their weapons) and could be required to march with their colors (flags) furled or cased - British General Charles Cornwallis is notably ABSENT: he claimed illness and sent Brigadier General Charles O'Hara in his place — O'Hara is visible at the head of the British column on horseback, conspicuously not Cornwallis; his expression is stiff with the humiliation of surrogate surrender - O'Hara first attempted to surrender his sword to Rochambeau (trying to surrender to the French rather than the Americans); Rochambeau gestured to Washington; Washington directed O'Hara to his own deputy Benjamin Lincoln — this snub and counter-snub is a small drama visible in the body language of the mounted officers at the center of the scene

    THE BRITISH SOLDIERS: - The British regulars march with relative discipline but their demeanors show the cost of the siege: some appear exhausted; some young soldiers' faces show shock and humiliation; the German (Hessian) mercenary regiments march in their distinctive dark blue uniforms with distinctive mitre-cap helmets; some soldiers are visibly looking toward the flanking allied troops

    HISTORICAL DETAILS OF THE SIEGE (VISIBLE IN BACKGROUND): - In the far background (left): the earthwork siege lines and parallel trenches that the allied forces dug methodically around the British fortifications over three weeks; the raw earth of the siege works is still visible; some cannon positions visible - Smoke rising in the background from the destroyed British fortifications inside the Yorktown perimeter — the 11-day allied artillery bombardment had been devastating - The York River visible in the far right background: the French fleet (Admiral de Grasse's 28 ships of the line) had blocked British naval escape and resupply — visible as ship masts on the horizon, French flags flying; without the French navy, there is no Yorktown victory

    THE NEGLECTED DIMENSION — THE BLACK LOYALISTS: - In the middle ground, behind the British lines: a small but visible group of formerly enslaved African Americans who had served with the British as laborers, guides, and soldiers (the Ethiopian Regiment and others); they walk with the British surrender column but their fate is grimly different — Washington demanded the return of formerly enslaved people who had fled to the British, and many would be re-enslaved; their expressions are afraid and uncertain

    OPTIONAL CAPTION (lower right, dark panel, white serif text): "October 19, 1781, Yorktown, Virginia. Cornwallis's 8,000-man army surrenders to Washington and Rochambeau. The French alliance — navy and army — was essential to this victory. Approximately 5,000 Black Loyalists who served the British were returned to enslavement after the surrender. Peace negotiations in Paris would take two more years (Treaty of Paris, 1783)."

    TEXTBOOK ILLUSTRATION STYLE: Grand historical painting in the tradition of John Trumbull and Eugène Lami — panoramic, with attention to military detail and historical nuance. Bright Virginia October afternoon light. Rich palette of white French uniforms, blue Continental coats, British red, golden autumn grass and foliage.
