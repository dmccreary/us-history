    A wide-format historical scene illustration (16:9 aspect ratio) depicting the autumn 1621 harvest gathering at Plymouth Colony between the Wampanoag people and the Separatist colonists — styled as a nuanced, historically revisionist US history textbook illustration that corrects common myths while honoring the documented historical event.

    COMPOSITION — OUTDOOR FEAST SCENE: A broad outdoor scene set at the edge of the Plymouth Colony settlement in late September or early October 1621 (the exact date is unknown — NOT necessarily in November). The scene spans the full width of the frame: a shared outdoor gathering space between the colonists' crude first-year structures on the left and the open forest/coastal scrubland on the right.

    LEFT AREA — PLYMOUTH COLONISTS: - Approximately 20–25 English Separatist colonists (about half the original Mayflower passengers survived to this gathering) - Clothing accuracy: plain Jacobean English dress — men in doublets or coarse linen shirts, breeches, stockings, practical felt hats (NOT tall Puritan hats with buckles — those are a 19th-century artistic invention); women in linen coifs (caps), plain wool kirtles (skirts), and linen aprons; children in smaller versions of adult clothing - Colors: muted — brown, gray, russet, deep green, off-white linen; NO black-and-white Pilgrim costumes — the colonists wore the everyday working clothes of English rural people of the 1620s - Several women tend cooking fires; men carry wooden trenchers (flat wooden plates) and simple pewter spoons - Background: three or four small timber-frame houses (crude first-year construction, wattle and daub with thatched roofs — NOT log cabins), a simple palisade fence, a cleared field with corn stalks

    CENTER — THE SHARED GATHERING: - Tables improvised from boards set on trestles, covered with rough linen cloth - Foods historically documented or plausible: roasted wild fowl (geese and ducks are more likely than turkeys — Governor Bradford mentioned fowling; Edward Winslow's account mentions "wild fowl"); venison (five deer brought by the Wampanoag); corn (maize) in several preparations; shellfish (mussels, clams — the coast was nearby); dried berries; squash; possibly eels (a Plymouth Colony staple) - NO cranberry sauce (sugar was nearly exhausted); NO pumpkin pie (they had no butter or wheat flour for pie crust); NO mashed potatoes (potatoes not yet in use by New England colonists) — these are anachronisms - The gathering feels informal and practical, not a formal seated banquet

    RIGHT AREA — WAMPANOAG PEOPLE: - Approximately 40–50 Wampanoag men — historically Edward Winslow's account says "about ninety of them" attended; the Wampanoag contingent significantly outnumbered the colonists - The Wampanoag figures are shown with historical accuracy: men in breechcloths and leggings appropriate to early autumn; some in deerskin mantles; traditional tattoos and body paint; hair styles accurate to 17th-century Wampanoag practice (shaved sides with a roach or top knot for warriors; longer styles for others) - NO Plains-style war bonnets or tipis — these are coastal Algonquian people - The Wampanoag are shown as HOSTS in their own territory, not guests — they controlled this land; the colonists were survivors of a terrible winter who had been aided by the Wampanoag all year (Squanto/Tisquantum, Samoset, and Massasoit Ousamequin's alliance were essential to Plymouth's survival) - Massasoit Ousamequin (the Wampanoag sachem/leader) is visible near center-right: a powerful man in his 40s, wearing a deerskin mantle with wampum decoration, standing with quiet authority - Wampanoag men are shown with their bows and muskets — historically, the three-day gathering involved military exercises and target shooting, not just eating

    SEASON AND SETTING: - Late September/early October New England: the leaves are just beginning to turn (gold, orange, russet) — gorgeous autumn light filtering through oak and maple trees - The Atlantic coast scrubland: bayberry, beach plum, oak, pine - A bright, clear autumn day — crisp air, long afternoon shadows - The ocean or a bay is visible in the distant background, conveying Plymouth's coastal location

    MOOD AND TONE: - The scene has warmth and genuine human connection — this was a real three-day event that both parties found meaningful in their own strategic and cultural terms - But it should not feel like a Norman Rockwell idealization — there is an underlying tension visible if you look: the Wampanoag people are evaluating their colonial neighbors; the colonists are acutely aware of how much they depend on Wampanoag goodwill; this alliance would shatter within 54 years in King Philip's War

    OPTIONAL CAPTION (lower right, dark panel, white serif text): "Autumn 1621, Plymouth Colony — A three-day harvest gathering, not a formal 'Thanksgiving.' The Wampanoag, who outnumbered the colonists 90 to 53, brought five deer. Within 54 years, King Philip's War would devastate both peoples. The 'First Thanksgiving' as a national myth was created in the 19th century."

    TEXTBOOK ILLUSTRATION STYLE: Painterly realism correcting the traditional Jennie Brownscombe / Jean Leon Gerome Ferris tradition — historically accurate clothing, food, numbers, and power dynamics. Warm New England autumn palette. Authoritative but accessible.
